# From Intern to Staff Engineer in {{TIMEFRAME_TITLE}} Years

*What an unusually fast career progression taught me about ownership, impact, and being useful.*

In June 2024, I started at SolarWinds as a Security Engineering Intern. A little over {{TIMEFRAME_BODY}} years later, I've accepted a position as a Staff Software Engineer on our AI Developer Platform team.

In between, I've held roles in DevOps, AI Operations, and now software engineering. It's been a pretty crazy couple of years, and certainly not a career trajectory I expected when I graduated college.

I want to be clear about something upfront: **I don't think I'm some exceptional engineer who figured out a secret formula for getting promoted.** I've been fortunate to have great mentors, supportive leadership, and opportunities to work on meaningful problems. A lot of people work just as hard without getting those same opportunities.

But I do think there are a handful of decisions, habits, and lessons that contributed to my progression, and I want to share them with anyone just getting started.

## 1. Be Useful

Early in my career, someone gave me a piece of advice that has stuck with me:

**You don't have to be the smartest person in the room. Be the most useful.** Thanks Josh - I think about this constantly.

Especially early in your career, it's easy to get caught up in trying to prove how much you know. You want to demonstrate your technical knowledge, use the newest technologies, and show everyone you belong.

But being knowledgeable and being useful aren't necessarily the same thing.

You can understand a system better than anyone else on your team, but if you never turn that knowledge into something actionable, what value are you actually providing?

On the other hand, you can be relatively inexperienced and still be incredibly valuable.

Be the person who volunteers to investigate an annoying recurring issue. The person who writes the missing documentation. The person who notices a manual process everyone hates and figures out how to automate it.

**Be a doer.**

That doesn't mean blindly saying yes to everything or working yourself into the ground. It means developing a habit of looking for problems you can actually solve, and then following through.

Early in your career, you probably won't have the expertise to tackle every complex architectural challenge. That's fine.

Start by making the people around you more effective.

## 2. Focus on Outcomes, Not Activities

One of the biggest shifts in how I approached my work was learning to think in terms of outcomes rather than completed tasks.

Consider these two statements:

- "I implemented improvements to our GitHub Actions workflows."
- "I reduced GitHub Actions runner minute consumption by approximately 45%."

Both could describe the same work, but the second communicates why it mattered.

During my time in DevOps, I worked on everything from software supply chain security to developer productivity. Some projects improved security coverage, others reduced infrastructure costs, and others removed friction from everyday development.

For example, I helped expand our CodeQL scanning coverage by roughly 400%, reduced CI/CD runner consumption by ~45%, and later worked on changes that reduced annual code signing costs from approximately $240,000 to $40,000.

The technologies involved were interesting, sure, but the actual value came from what those changes accomplished.

This is something I encourage newer engineers to think about when choosing projects, presenting work, or even writing their resumes.

Before starting something, ask yourself: **What will be better when this is finished?**

Will a process be faster? Will something cost less? Will developers spend less time waiting? Will a system become more reliable or secure?

If you can't explain why a project matters, it's worth reconsidering whether you're solving the right problem.

## 3. Ownership Is More Than Writing Code

As I progressed, I started realizing that writing the code was often the easiest part of delivering a solution.

The harder part was identifying the problem, understanding the constraints, coordinating with the people involved, and making sure the solution actually worked for its intended users.

Take an internal self-service platform I worked on in DevOps.

The goal wasn't simply to build another application. It was to eliminate unnecessary manual work from common engineering requests, things like repository creation, access management, and cloud resource provisioning.

Solving that problem meant thinking beyond implementation. It meant understanding how requests flowed between teams, where bottlenecks existed, and what could safely be automated.

I've found that **the more responsibility you're willing and able to take for an outcome, the more opportunities you create for yourself.**

There's a difference between someone who says "my part is finished" and someone who asks "did we actually solve the problem?"

You don't need a senior title to start thinking that way.

And importantly, ownership doesn't mean doing everything yourself. Knowing when to involve someone with more experience, ask for help, or delegate is part of doing the job well.

## 4. Learn to Communicate With Everyone

You can be a fantastic technical engineer, but if you struggle to communicate what you're doing, why it matters, or what you need from other people, you'll eventually hit a ceiling.

I've written about this before in [Communicating With Executives](https://chaseroohms.com/blog/communicationg-with-executives/), but the principle applies far beyond leadership.

Different audiences care about different things.

An engineer might want to understand the technical implementation. A security team might care about risk. A manager might need to understand the timeline, dependencies, and expected impact.

Being able to communicate the same project appropriately to each of those audiences is incredibly valuable.

As my work expanded into AI Operations, this became especially important.

I was working across IT, Engineering, Security, and other parts of the organization to help scale AI adoption. Success depended on more than having the right technical platform. It required understanding the concerns and priorities of different teams and bringing everyone toward a practical solution.

One thing I've learned is that people don't necessarily need you to have every answer. They need to trust that you'll be transparent, communicate problems early, and follow through on what you say you're going to do.

## 5. Don't Limit Your Learning to Your Job Description

I've always enjoyed building things outside of work.

Home servers, Discord bots, automation scripts, and more recently [Transmute](https://transmute.sh), my open-source file converter.

Not every project has been particularly useful, and plenty of them have been unnecessarily complicated. But they've all taught me something.

Building Transmute, in particular, exposed me to responsibilities I wouldn't necessarily encounter in my day-to-day work.

Beyond designing and developing the application, I've had to think about documentation, releases, user experience, security, community feedback, and managing contributions from other developers.

It's one thing to build software that works on your machine. It's another to maintain something that thousands of people might depend on.

I don't think everyone needs a successful open-source project or a side hustle to advance their career. Your free time is yours, and there's nothing wrong with spending it doing something completely unrelated to technology.

But I do encourage engineers who enjoy tinkering to keep building things.

**You learn an incredible amount when there's nobody handing you a ticket with the solution already outlined.**

## 6. Be Willing to Move Outside Your Comfort Zone

My career so far hasn't followed a particularly straight line. I started in security, moved into DevOps, transitioned into AI Operations, and am now joining an engineering organization focused on AI developer platforms.

Each transition brought new technologies, new responsibilities, and plenty of things I didn't know, and that's been a good thing.

Moving between disciplines helped me understand how different parts of an engineering organization fit together.

Security taught me to think about risk and controls. DevOps reinforced the importance of reliability, automation, and developer experience. AI Operations pushed me toward platform adoption, cost management, and measuring whether technology was actually delivering value. These experiences build on each other.

My advice isn't to change jobs every few months or constantly chase whatever technology is trending, it's to avoid becoming so comfortable with what you already know that you stop pursuing opportunities to grow.

Sometimes the most valuable thing you can do for your career is accept a challenge you're not entirely sure you're ready for.

Just be willing to put in the work to become ready.

## 7. Your Reputation Is Built on Follow-Through

This ties back to being useful - over time, I think one of the biggest career advantages you can develop is a reputation for being dependable.

- When someone gives you a difficult problem, do they trust that you'll make progress on it?
- When something goes wrong, do you communicate it or hope nobody notices?
- When you don't know something, are you willing to admit it and find someone who does?

Those behaviors might not look particularly impressive on a resume, but they make an enormous difference in how people experience working with you.

I've been incredibly fortunate to work with people who trusted me with responsibilities I hadn't held before. That trust opened doors, but it also came with an obligation to deliver.

**Every time you follow through, you're building a little more credibility for the next opportunity.**

And when the next opportunity comes around, you want people to already feel confident putting your name forward.

## So, How Do You Get to Staff Engineer?

I don't think there's a universal answer, and I'd be skeptical of anyone claiming there is. Engineering titles aren't standardized between companies. A Staff Engineer at one organization might have very different responsibilities from one at another.

I also recognize that my progression has been unusually fast. Timing, organizational needs, the people advocating for you, and plain luck all play a role.

I'm especially grateful to the leaders and mentors who recognized my potential, gave me room to grow, and were willing to take a chance on me.

And while I've accepted a Staff Engineer position, I certainly don't consider that proof I've mastered everything the role requires. Quite the opposite. I have a lot to learn, and I'm excited to learn it.

But if I could go back to June 2024 and give myself a few pieces of advice, they'd be pretty simple:

1. **Be useful.** Look for meaningful problems and help solve them.
2. **Own outcomes.** Don't confuse completing a task with delivering value.
3. **Communicate clearly.** Make it easy for people to understand and trust your work.
4. **Keep learning.** Especially when the opportunity feels a little intimidating.
5. **Follow through.** Your reputation is built over hundreds of small interactions.

Getting promoted is exciting, and I'm incredibly thankful for the opportunities I've had, but the title was never really the goal.

The goal was to become the kind of engineer people could count on to solve difficult problems.

That's what I intend to keep working toward.
